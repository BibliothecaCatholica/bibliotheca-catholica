Book of the Week: Bede's Ecclesiastical History of the English People

How the Faith came to England, told by a monk who named his sources

St. Bede the Venerable, a monk of Jarrow and a Doctor of the Church, finished his Ecclesiastical History in 731. Across five books he tells how the Gospel reached Britain and the English. His story runs from Julius Caesar's arrival, through Pope Gregory's mission to Kent, to the saints, kings, and councils of his own day.

Why read it. It is a history full of saints: Augustine of Canterbury, Aidan, Hilda, and Cuthbert. It is also a model of careful method, because Bede tells the reader where his information came from. And it is full of memorable scenes, such as King Oswald raising a wooden cross before battle.

Where to begin. Start with the short preface, where Bede lists his sources. Then read Book I, chapters 23 to 26, on Augustine's mission, and Book III, chapters 1 to 6, on Oswald and Aidan.

Which edition. The library's free text is A. M. Sellar's revised translation (1907). It has a helpful introduction, a life of Bede, and notes that identify places and people. That makes it easy to follow.

One question to carry. Bede says history moves the reader "to imitate that which is good." Which figure in the story would you most like to imitate?

For if history relates good things of good men, the attentive hearer is excited to imitate that which is good; or if it recounts evil things of wicked persons, none the less the conscientious and devout hearer or reader, shunning that which is hurtful and wrong, is the more earnestly fired to perform those things which he knows to be good, and worthy of the service of God.

— Bede, Ecclesiastical History, Preface; trans. A. M. Sellar (London: George Bell and Sons, 1907)
