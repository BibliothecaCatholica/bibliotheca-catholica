Book of the Week: Newman's An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine

The book Newman was still printing when he became a Catholic

In 1845, St. John Henry Newman was still an Anglican and was writing a book to settle a question that troubled him. Did Catholic doctrine as he saw it in his own day faithfully grow from the faith of the apostles, or had it been corrupted? While the book was at the printer, he reached his answer and joined the Catholic Church. A postscript, written afterwards, tells the story.

Why read it. Newman takes seriously the fact that doctrine has a history. He argues that a living idea unfolds over time. He then proposes seven "Notes" for telling a true development from a corruption, among them preservation of type and continuity of principles. It is still the classic treatment of the question.

Where to begin. Read the short Advertisement and Postscript first. Then read Chapter I, section 1, on how ideas develop, which ends with the famous line quoted below. Then turn to Chapter V for the seven Notes.

Which edition. The library's free text is the sixth edition. For print, the site's edition guide recommends the University of Notre Dame Press reprint.

One question to carry. Newman says a true development "changes... in order to remain the same." What in your own faith has deepened over the years without becoming something else?

It changes with them in order to remain the same. In a higher world it is otherwise, but here below to live is to change, and to be perfect is to have changed often.

— St. John Henry Newman, An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine, ch. I, sect. 1, §7; sixth edition, Project Gutenberg #35110

Liturgical note. In the last days of Advent, the Church's prayer gathers the prophecies of many centuries into one cry for the Messiah. Newman's book is about that same long unfolding: one truth, received and understood more deeply over time.
