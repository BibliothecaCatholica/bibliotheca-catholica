Book of the Week: The Consolation of Philosophy

Boethius's prison dialogue on fortune, happiness, and providence

Boethius was a Roman statesman and a Christian. He was imprisoned on charges he denied, wrote this book while awaiting death, and was executed in 524. In it Lady Philosophy visits his cell and talks him back from despair. The book alternates prose and verse, and it was among the most widely read books of the Middle Ages.

Why read it. It takes the oldest complaint seriously: why do the wicked prosper and the good suffer? And it answers by reasoning, step by step, from Fortune's wheel to true happiness to God's providence.

Where to begin. Start with Book II, where Fortune defends herself. Then go on to Book IV, prose 6, on providence and fate, and Book V, prose 6, on eternity and free will. These connect directly to this week's essay and explorations.

Which edition. The library's free text is H. R. James's translation (1897), which renders the verse as verse. Its English is older but clear. If you buy a modern translation, choose one that keeps the poems.

One question to carry. Lady Philosophy says no one can take from you what is truly yours. What, in your life, could Fortune not take away?

Liturgical note. In the second week of Advent, the Church asks us to wait in hope. Boethius waited in a prison cell and found that hope does not depend on circumstances. He is a companion for anyone whose waiting is hard this year.
