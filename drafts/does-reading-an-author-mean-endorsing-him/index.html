Does Reading an Author Mean Endorsing Him?

St. Paul quotes a pagan poet, and St. Thomas explains why that is not a compliment to paganism

Many readers carry a quiet worry: if I read this author, quote him, or recommend him, am I signing off on everything he believed? The worry is understandable. But the Catholic tradition has a clear and useful distinction here, and one passage of St. Thomas Aquinas states it better than any other.

Paul in Athens

The example Thomas chooses comes from Scripture itself. When St. Paul preached on the Areopagus, he quoted a Greek poet to his pagan audience. In the words Thomas cites: "As some also of your own poets said: For we are also His offspring" (Acts 17:28). The poet, Aratus, was no Christian. Paul quoted him anyway, because on this point he was right.

Thomas's distinction

In the first question of the Summa Theologiae, Thomas asks whether sacred doctrine argues at all. In answering, he sorts the authorities a theologian may cite into three kinds, and gives each a different weight:

Nevertheless, sacred doctrine makes use of these authorities as extrinsic and probable arguments; but properly uses the authority of the canonical Scriptures as an incontrovertible proof, and the authority of the doctors of the Church as one that may properly be used, yet merely as probable.

— St. Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 1, a. 8, ad 2

So there are three levels:

  1. Philosophers (Aristotle, Plato, Aratus): used "in those questions in which they were able to know the truth by natural reason," as outside, probable support.
  2. The doctors of the Church (Augustine, Jerome, and the rest): used properly, but still "merely as probable."
  3. The canonical Scriptures: "an incontrovertible proof."

Thomas then gives the reason: "our faith rests upon the revelation made to the apostles and prophets who wrote the canonical books, and not on the revelations (if any such there are) made to other doctors."

Notice what this does. Citing Aristotle on a question of natural reason does not make Aristotle an authority on the Trinity. And even citing a saint does not make his every opinion binding. Each source is weighed for what it can actually bear.

Agreeing in part

St. Justin Martyr, writing in the second century, describes the same distinction from the side of a convert. He had loved Plato. Why, then, become a Christian?

not because the teachings of Plato are different from those of Christ, but because they are not in all respects similar

— St. Justin Martyr, Second Apology, ch. 13

"Not in all respects similar" is exactly the reader's position. We can hold that Plato saw something true about the good, and also that he was wrong about other things, without contradiction.

Augustine shows what this looks like in practice. In Book VIII of The City of God he can write, "It is evident that none come nearer to us than the Platonists." Yet the surrounding books are an extended argument with those same Platonists about worship and mediation. Praise and disagreement sit in the same pages, because he is judging each claim, not the man as a whole.

A working rule

Put together, the tradition suggests a simple habit:

  • Ask what kind of question this is. Is the author speaking where natural reason, observation, or experience can reach? Then his argument can be weighed on its merits.
  • Weigh the source for what it can bear. A philosopher's argument, a saint's opinion, and a text of Scripture do not carry the same weight, and Thomas says so explicitly.
  • Receive the true and set aside the rest. Augustine's counsel is to recognize and acknowledge the truth "even in their religious literature," and to "reject the figments of superstition."

None of this means every book is wise to read at every moment. Prudence about what to read, and when, is a real virtue, and a confessor or spiritual director may counsel a particular person differently. But reading an author, and even learning from him, is not the same as endorsing him.
