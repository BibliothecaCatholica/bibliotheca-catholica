"Everywhere, Always, by All": What Vincent's Rule Does and Doesn't Settle

The most quoted sentence about Tradition, and Newman's honest look at its limits

If you have read anything about Catholic Tradition, you have probably met the phrase: the faith is what has been believed "everywhere, always, by all." It comes from St. Vincent of Lérins, a fifth-century monk. It is often quoted as if it settled every question. This exploration asks a narrower one: what exactly did Vincent's rule claim, and what can it actually do?

The rule itself

Vincent wrote his Commonitory as a guide for telling true doctrine from heresy. In chapter 2 he states his principle:

Moreover, in the Catholic Church itself, all possible care must be taken, that we hold that faith which has been believed everywhere, always, by all.

— St. Vincent of Lérins, Commonitory, ch. 2

He immediately turns it into three tests. We follow universality "if we confess that one faith to be true, which the whole Church throughout the world confesses." We follow antiquity if we do not depart from the interpretations "notoriously held by our holy ancestors and fathers." And we follow consent if, within antiquity, we adhere to the agreed teaching "of all, or at the least of almost all priests and doctors."

Notice that small qualification: "or at the least of almost all." Even Vincent did not imagine a test requiring unanimous written testimony from every bishop who ever lived.

What the rule does well

The rule is powerful against novelty. If someone proposes a doctrine that no church held, that no early Father taught, and that the Church as a whole never confessed, Vincent's tests expose it quickly. That was his purpose: to protect the faithful from new teachings presented as ancient.

What it cannot do on its own

The difficulty comes when we try to use the rule positively, as a machine for producing a complete list of doctrines. John Henry Newman examined exactly this in the introduction to his Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine (1845). He grants that the rule "contains a majestic truth, and offers an intelligible principle." But, he says, "its difficulty lies in applying it in particular cases. The rule is more serviceable in determining what is not, than what is Christianity."

Newman then quotes an earlier writer, in fact himself, from his Anglican years, who had already seen the problem:

what is meant by being 'taught always'? does it mean in every century, or every year, or every month? Does 'everywhere' mean in every country, or in every diocese?

— Newman, An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine, Introduction, quoting his Prophetical Office

The rule, that earlier writer concluded, "is not of a mathematical or demonstrative character, but moral, and requires practical judgment and good sense to apply it."

Vincent's own second step

Vincent himself did not leave the matter there. Later in the Commonitory he asks whether there can be any progress in the Church. His answer is: "Certainly; all possible progress." But it must be "real progress, not alteration of the faith." Understanding should increase "in its own kind; that is to say, in the same doctrine, in the same sense, and in the same meaning."

So Vincent's teaching has two halves. The famous rule protects what has always been believed. The principle of growth allows that what has always been believed can be understood more deeply and stated more fully over time. Newman's Essay is, in large part, an attempt to explain how to tell that kind of growth from corruption.

What this means for a reader

When you see "everywhere, always, by all" quoted, it is worth remembering three things:

  1. It is a strong test against new doctrines, which is what Vincent designed it for.
  2. It needs judgment to apply, as Vincent's own "almost all" and Newman's questions show.
  3. Vincent paired it with a principle of real growth in understanding.

It is also worth being clear about status. Vincent's rule is a Father's guide, widely respected and often cited. It is not itself a definition of faith. The Church's teaching on how Tradition is handed on and grows is set out in Dei Verbum (nos. 8 to 10).
