How Did Bede Know?

Reading the preface, and one chapter, of the Ecclesiastical History for the way Bede signals his sources

The Venerable Bede wrote about events that happened, in some cases, a century or more before he was born. How could he know them? This exploration does not try to judge every story in the Ecclesiastical History. It asks something narrower: how does Bede tell us where his information comes from, so that we can judge for ourselves?

The preface: a list of sources

Bede dedicates the History to King Ceolwulf of Northumbria. Partway through the dedication he turns to the question of sources, and gives his reason for doing so: "to the end that I may remove all occasion of doubting what I have written, both from yourself and other readers or hearers of this history, I will take care briefly to show you from what authors I chiefly learned the same."

What follows is, in effect, a map of his evidence, region by region:

  • Kent and the mission of Pope Gregory's disciples: Abbot Albinus of Canterbury, sending material through the London priest Nothelm, "either in writing, or by word of mouth."
  • Papal letters: Nothelm went to Rome and "searched into the archives of the Holy Roman Church," bringing back "some epistles of the blessed Pope Gregory, and other popes."
  • The West Saxons, South Saxons, and the Isle of Wight: Bishop Daniel, "who is still living," who sent him information in writing.
  • The Mercians and East Saxons: "the brethren of the monastery" founded by Cedd and Ceadda, at Lastingham.
  • The East Angles: "the writings and tradition of former men," and "the account of the most reverend Abbot Esi."
  • Lindsey: "the letters of the most reverend prelate Cynibert, or by word of mouth from other persons of good credit."
  • Northumbria, his own region: "not on the authority of any one man, but by the faithful testimony of innumerable witnesses," besides "what I had of my own knowledge."

That is a remarkable thing for an eighth-century monk to have done. He separates written documents from oral reports, names his informants, notes which are still alive, and distinguishes what he knows firsthand.

The same habits inside the stories

Bede keeps these signals going in the narrative itself. Take Book III, chapter 2, the story of King Oswald raising a wooden cross before the battle at Heavenfield. Read it closely, and you can see three different kinds of claim:

  1. What he has been told: "Then, we are told, that the cross being made in haste... the king himself... laid hold of it and held it upright with both his hands." The phrase "we are told" marks a report handed down to him.
  2. What can still be seen: "The place is shown to this day, and held in much veneration." That is a present-day fact his readers could check.
  3. A named, living witness: For a healing at the cross, he names the man: "One of the brothers of the same church of Hagulstald, whose name is Bothelm, and who is still living."

Bede believed in miracles, and he reports them without embarrassment. But he does not present a story about a battle long ago in the same way as a cure he could attribute to a man his readers might meet. He lets the reader see the difference.

Bede's own standard

The preface ends with a phrase historians still quote. Bede asks the reader not to blame him for any errors, "for, as the true rule of history requires, withholding nothing, I have laboured to commit to writing such things as I could gather from common report, for the instruction of posterity."

"Common report" is an honest admission. Some of what he records is what people said. By telling us so, Bede gives us the tools to weigh it.

What this does, and doesn't, settle

Knowing Bede's sources does not prove every story true. A named informant can be mistaken, and a report can grow in the retelling. But it shows a historian who wanted to be checked, and that is the first mark of one worth trusting.
