How Do We Know What Happened?

Eusebius, Bede, and a Jesuit scholar on reading Christian history as evidence

Christianity is a religion of events. The Creed names a Roman governor. The Gospels give us places, rulers, and the names of witnesses. The faith does not float above history; it claims that certain things really happened.

That is why Christians have always cared about evidence. If the faith rests on events, it matters how we know about them. It also matters how we read the many later stories that have gathered around those events: chronicles, saints' lives, legends, and pious traditions. Some are history of the first order. Some are devotion dressed as history. A thoughtful Catholic reader needs to tell the difference without becoming either credulous or cynical.

Three writers in the library, spread across sixteen centuries, show how.

Eusebius: the first to walk the road

Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 260–339) set out to write a history of the Church from the apostles to his own day. He knew he was doing something new, and he said so:

since I am the first to enter upon the subject, I am attempting to traverse as it were a lonely and untrodden path.

— Eusebius, Church History, Book I, ch. 1

Eusebius did not have a tradition of Church historians to rely on. What he had were "brief fragments": letters, lists of bishops, and earlier writings. He describes them beautifully: "From afar they raise their voices like torches." His method, which he follows throughout the book, was to gather those voices, quote them, and let the reader see the sources for himself. Much of what we know about the first three Christian centuries survives only because Eusebius copied it.

Bede: naming his witnesses

Four centuries later, in a monastery in the north of England, the Venerable Bede finished his Ecclesiastical History of the English People. He opens it with a preface to King Ceolwulf that is, in effect, a list of sources.

He explains why he is giving one: "to the end that I may remove all occasion of doubting what I have written." Then he names names. His "principal authority and aid" was Abbot Albinus of Canterbury. A London priest named Nothelm searched the papal archives in Rome and brought back letters of Pope Gregory. Bishop Daniel of the West Saxons sent written information. For events in Northumbria, Bede relied not on one man but on "the faithful testimony of innumerable witnesses," as well as his own knowledge.

Bede closes the preface with a request that every honest historian could make:

And I humbly entreat the reader, that if he shall find in these our writings anything not delivered according to the truth, he will not lay the blame of it on me, for, as the true rule of history requires, withholding nothing, I have laboured to commit to writing such things as I could gather from common report, for the instruction of posterity.

— Bede, Ecclesiastical History, Preface

Notice two things. First, Bede is transparent: he tells you where his information came from, so you can weigh it. Second, he is modest: he reports what his sources told him, including miracle stories, and he does not claim more certainty than he has. (This week's short exploration looks at his preface more closely.)

Delehaye: history and hagiography

Twelve centuries after Bede, a Belgian Jesuit named Hippolyte Delehaye took up a harder question. He belonged to the Bollandists, a society of scholars devoted to studying the lives of the saints. He wrote a short book in French, Légendes hagiographiques, which was translated into English in 1907 as The Legends of the Saints. It begins with a distinction:

The point to be emphasised from the first is the distinction between hagiography and history. The work of the hagiographer may be historical, but it is not necessarily so.

— Hippolyte Delehaye, S.J., The Legends of the Saints, ch. I

A saint's life written to edify the faithful is a work of devotion. It may preserve real history, or it may be closer to a poem or a legend. Delehaye knew that some devout readers would hear this as an attack on the saints. He answered that the opposite was true. People accused careful scholars of rationalism, he wrote, "as though in questions of fact it were not above all things essential to weigh the evidence." And sorting the reliable from the unreliable does not diminish the saints:

To give assistance in detecting materials of inferior workmanship is not to deny the excellence of what remains, and it is to the ultimate advantage of the harvest to point out the tares that have sometimes become mingled with the wheat to a most disconcerting extent.

— Delehaye, The Legends of the Saints, Author's Introduction

Four questions to ask of any source

Taken together, these writers suggest a few plain questions for reading any historical claim, whether in an old chronicle or a modern article:

  1. Who says so? Eusebius quotes his sources. Bede names his.
  2. How could they know? Were they eyewitnesses, or did they learn it from others? Bede distinguishes what he gathered from documents, from informants, and "from common report."
  3. When was it written? A letter from the time carries a different weight from a story written down centuries later.
  4. What kind of writing is it? As Delehaye insists, a sermon, a legend, and a chronicle are not trying to do the same thing.

These questions are not hostile to faith. They are what it looks like to take the truth seriously.

Why this matters now

These are not only questions for scholars. Every week, quotations attributed to saints circulate online with no source at all. Some are genuine. Many are paraphrases that have hardened into quotations, and some were never said. Stories about the early Church are retold with confidence and without a footnote.

The library exists partly to help with exactly this: to put the texts themselves within reach, so that readers can check what a Father or a saint actually wrote. Eusebius and Bede would recognize the instinct. Both wanted their readers to be able to see where the story came from. Delehaye wanted the same, and he thought the saints had nothing to fear from it.

What the Church teaches, and what this essay argues

Church teaching. The Second Vatican Council's Dei Verbum (no. 19) teaches that the Church firmly holds the historical character of the four Gospels. They faithfully hand on what Jesus really did and taught. The Council also notes that the evangelists selected and arranged material from what had been handed down, while always telling the honest truth about Jesus. The faith is bound to real events.

What is not Church teaching. The particular details of later chronicles and saints' legends are not, as such, articles of faith. Delehaye's book is a scholar's work, not a magisterial document, and Bede's and Eusebius's judgments are those of historians. They can be right or wrong on a given point.

This essay's argument. The four questions above are my own summary, drawn from these writers, of a sensible way to read. They are offered as a practical aid, not as a rule the Church has laid down.

Where to start

Read Bede's preface; it takes ten minutes. Then read Book I, chapter 1 of Eusebius. Delehaye's Author's Introduction is short and is a good next step. This week's Book of the Week introduces Bede's History as a whole.
