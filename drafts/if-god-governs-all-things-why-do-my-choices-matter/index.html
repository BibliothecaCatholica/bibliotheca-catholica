If God Governs All Things, Why Do My Choices Matter?

Boethius in prison, Augustine against the fatalists, and the dignity of being a real cause

Most of us have had the thought at some point, usually late at night. If God already knows everything that will happen, and if nothing escapes His providence, then what difference does my choice make? Why agonize over a decision, or pray about it, if the outcome is already in His hands?

The question can lead in two bad directions. One is a quiet fatalism: whatever will be, will be, so effort and prayer are a kind of theatre. The other is to shrink God down to make room for us, imagining Him as a spectator who set the world going and now waits to see what we do. The Catholic tradition refuses both. It holds, at the same time, that God governs all things and that our choices are truly ours. The writers who have helped me most to see how those fit together are a condemned man in a sixth-century prison, a bishop arguing with Cicero, and a friar who gave the whole matter a single phrase: the dignity of causality.

A man waiting for execution

In the early sixth century, the Roman statesman Boethius was imprisoned on charges he insisted were false; he was put to death in 524. While he awaited execution, he wrote The Consolation of Philosophy, a dialogue between himself and Lady Philosophy, who visits his cell.

Boethius begins where many of us begin when life goes wrong: with the feeling that the world is ruled by luck. Lady Philosophy lets Fortune speak for herself:

This is my art, this the game I never cease to play. I turn the wheel that spins. I delight to see the high come down and the low ascend.

— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book II

If Fortune were the last word, there would be little to say. But Philosophy leads him past the wheel to something steadier. In Book IV she distinguishes two ways of looking at the same order of events:

Providence is the Divine reason itself, seated in the Supreme Being, which disposes all things; fate is the disposition inherent in all things which move, through which providence joins all things in their proper order.

— Consolation, Book IV

What we experience as a chain of causes unfolding in time, one thing after another, God sees as a single, ordered whole. Boethius compares it to a craftsman who forms the whole design in his mind before carrying it out step by step. The steps are real. But they are not random, and they are not the whole story.

"We embrace both"

A century earlier, St. Augustine had faced the same question in a sharper form. Cicero, the great Roman orator, had argued that if God foreknows everything, then everything happens by necessity and the will cannot be free. To save freedom, Cicero was willing to deny that the future could be known at all.

Augustine thought that was too high a price. In Book V of The City of God he answers:

Therefore we are by no means compelled, either, retaining the prescience of God, to take away the freedom of the will, or, retaining the freedom of the will, to deny that He is prescient of future things, which is impious. But we embrace both. We faithfully and sincerely confess both. The former, that we may believe well; the latter, that we may live well.

— St. Augustine, The City of God, Book V, ch. 10

That last line is worth slowing down for. We confess God's foreknowledge "that we may believe well": a God who did not know the future would not be God. We confess free will "that we may live well": without it, repentance, effort, and love would be meaningless. Augustine does not claim to have dissolved every difficulty. He refuses to buy a tidy answer by giving up either truth.

The dignity of causality

St. Thomas Aquinas, writing in the thirteenth century, offers a way of seeing why the two truths need not compete. When he asks whether God governs everything directly, he answers that God has the plan of all things, down to the smallest, in His own mind. But in carrying it out, God works through creatures:

He governs things inferior by superior, not on account of any defect in His power, but by reason of the abundance of His goodness; so that the dignity of causality is imparted even to creatures.

— St. Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 22, a. 3

God does not use us because He needs help. He uses us because He is generous. He wants creatures to share in His work, as real causes and not as puppets. In the next article Thomas adds that providence does not make everything necessary. God has "prepared for some things necessary causes, so that they happen of necessity; for others contingent causes, that they may happen by contingency." Our free choices belong to that second kind. Providence does not override them. It is the reason they exist.

Seen this way, the question "If God governs all things, why do my choices matter?" turns inside out. My choices matter because God governs all things, and has chosen to govern part of the world through me.

What this changes

If that is right, it changes how we pray and how we act.

Prayer is not wasted. At the very end of the Consolation, Lady Philosophy says plainly: "Our hopes and prayers also are not fixed on God in vain, and when they are rightly directed cannot fail of effect." Prayer is one of the ways God has chosen to make us causes in His providence.

Responsibility is real. The final sentence of the book is not comfort but a charge: "Great is the necessity of righteousness laid upon you if ye will not hide it from yourselves, seeing that all your actions are done before the eyes of a Judge who seeth all things." If my choices are real, so is my accountability for them.

Anxiety can loosen. Trusting providence is not the same as being passive. It means doing the next right thing with care, and then leaving the outcome to the One who sees the whole design.

Boethius was not rescued. His conviction that providence ruled did not get him out of prison, and he never claimed it would. What it gave him was a place to stand.

What the Church teaches, and what this essay argues

Church teaching. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (paragraphs 302 to 314) teaches that God guides all creation by His providence. It also teaches that God makes use of His creatures' cooperation, not out of weakness but out of goodness. He gives them the dignity of acting on their own and of being causes, and He gives human beings the power to share freely in His providence. It teaches, too, that God is in no way the cause of moral evil, though He permits it and can draw good from it.

Theological explanation. Boethius's distinction between providence and fate, and Thomas's account of secondary causes, are explanations offered by great Christian thinkers. They are widely respected and deeply traditional. They are not themselves definitions of faith.

This essay's argument. The practical conclusions above, that prayer is one of the ways God makes us causes, and that trust in providence should loosen anxiety without breeding passivity, are my own application. They are offered for your reflection.

Where to start

Read Book IV, prose 6 and Book V, prose 6 of the Consolation. Together they take about half an hour. Then read City of God Book V, chapters 9 and 10. This week's Book of the Week introduces the Consolation as a whole.
