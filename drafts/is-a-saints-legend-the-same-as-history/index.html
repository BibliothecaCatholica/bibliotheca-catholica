Is a Saint's Legend the Same as History?

What the word "legend" first meant, and how a Jesuit scholar taught us to read one

The library holds a great many saints' lives, from Jacobus de Voragine's Golden Legend to Butler's and Baring-Gould's collections. Readers sometimes ask, a little anxiously: are these stories true? Is it irreverent to notice, as Delehaye himself did, that the legend of St. Dymphna echoes the old folk tale of "the ass's skin"? This exploration takes one narrow question, what a legend actually is, and answers it through one source: Hippolyte Delehaye's The Legends of the Saints (English translation, 1907).

"Legend" first meant "what is to be read"

We hear "legend" and think "made up." Delehaye points out that the word began somewhere else entirely. In its original sense, he writes, the legend "is the history that has to be read, legenda, on the feast of a saint." It was simply the reading for the day, the account of a martyr's passion or a confessor's life, "without reference to its historical value."

By the thirteenth century, he notes, the Golden Legend had made the word cover every kind of saint's life. So when a medieval reader called a book a "legend," he was not confessing that it was fiction. He was naming its use in prayer.

Two ingredients

To avoid confusion, Delehaye then narrows the word. In his book, "legend" will mean only "stories or incidents unauthenticated by history." And he explains how such stories arise. A legend, he says, presupposes "an historical fact as basis or pretext." That fact "may either be developed or disfigured by popular imagination." The two ingredients can be mixed "in very unequal proportions." Depending on whether fact or fiction predominates, "the narrative may be classed as history or as legend."

This is the key idea. Most saints' lives are not simply true or simply false. They are a real person, often a real martyrdom or a real holy life, to which imagination has added more or less ornament over time.

Hagiography is not trying to be history

Delehaye's most important distinction comes at the start of his book:

The point to be emphasised from the first is the distinction between hagiography and history. The work of the hagiographer may be historical, but it is not necessarily so.

— Delehaye, The Legends of the Saints, ch. I

A saint's life is written, as he says, to inspire devotion and edify the faithful. It can take "any literary form suitable to the glorification of the saints," from an official record to "a poetical composition of the most exuberant character wholly detached from reality." To ask of every saint's life whether it is accurate history is a little like asking whether a hymn is a newspaper report.

So how should we read them?

Delehaye's answer is neither "believe everything" nor "believe nothing."

  • Weigh the evidence for each story. Whether a given detail is historical is, in his words, "a point to be determined in each individual case."
  • Don't confuse doubting a legend with doubting a saint. He complains of readers who think that questioning a biographer means "attacking the saint himself."
  • Value what is genuine all the more. The early accounts written by eyewitnesses, he says, "deserve our fullest admiration and respect," and that is exactly why they must be distinguished from later embellishments.

It is worth adding a clear line. The Church venerates the saints and celebrates their feasts. But the particular details of a medieval saint's life are not, as such, part of the Catholic faith. Delehaye himself was a Jesuit priest who spent his life in this work, and he saw careful sorting as a service to the saints, not a threat. (That is a description of his scholarly position, not a statement of Church teaching.)
