Tradition or Traditions?

A distinction worth keeping, with help from St. Basil and the Catechism

Catholics argue a good deal about "tradition." Often, though, they are not using the word in the same way. One person means the apostolic faith itself. Another means the way Mass was celebrated in his grandmother's parish, a particular devotion, or a custom of fasting. This exploration asks one narrow question: what is the difference between Tradition and traditions, and why does it matter?

The Catechism's distinction

The Catechism of the Catholic Church draws the line plainly in paragraph 83. The Tradition it speaks of comes from the apostles. It hands on what they received from Jesus' teaching and example and what they learned from the Holy Spirit.

That Tradition, the Catechism says, is to be distinguished from the various theological, disciplinary, liturgical, or devotional traditions that have arisen in local churches over time. These are the particular forms, suited to different places and ages, in which the great Tradition is expressed. In the light of Tradition, and under the guidance of the Church's teaching office, they can be kept, modified, or even abandoned.

So there are two levels:

  • Tradition (capital T): the apostolic deposit of faith. It is handed on with Scripture and is not ours to change.
  • traditions (small t): the many customs, forms, and practices through which that faith has been lived. They are valuable and often ancient, but they are adaptable.

Basil: unwritten customs as witnesses

St. Basil the Great, writing On the Holy Spirit in the fourth century, shows why the unwritten side of the Church's life matters. He was defending a form of words in the Church's prayer that some opponents rejected because it was not found in Scripture. His reply was to point to practices everyone already accepted without a written command:

For instance, to take the first and most general example, who is there who has taught us in writing to sign with the sign of the cross those who have trusted in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ? What writing has taught us to turn to the East at the prayer?

— St. Basil, On the Holy Spirit, ch. 27

Basil goes further. Of the beliefs and practices preserved in the Church, he says, "some we possess derived from written teaching; others we have received delivered to us 'in a mystery' by the tradition of the apostles." If we tried to reject every custom lacking written authority, he warns, "we should unintentionally injure the Gospel in its very vitals."

Basil's point is that the Church's lived practice carries the faith. Her prayers, her gestures, and the way she baptizes and worships are not decoration added to doctrine. They bear witness to it. That is exactly why the distinction matters. Some practices witness to something unchangeable, and some are particular forms of that witness.

Why the distinction helps

Keeping the two levels apart guards against two opposite mistakes.

The first mistake treats every custom as untouchable. If every inherited practice is Tradition, then any change, even one the Church herself makes, looks like betrayal. The Catechism says otherwise: small-t traditions can be modified under the guidance of the Church.

The second mistake treats the faith itself as a custom. If everything is "just tradition," then doctrine handed down from the apostles can seem as negotiable as a parish schedule. That is also wrong. The apostolic Tradition is not ours to revise.

Most real questions fall somewhere between, and that is where prudence and the judgment of the Church come in. But simply asking "Is this Tradition, or a tradition?" clears away a great deal of confusion.

A note of caution

It is not always obvious which level a particular practice belongs to. The Catechism gives the principle, not a complete list. Sorting particular cases belongs to the Church's teaching office and to careful theology, not to individual preference.
