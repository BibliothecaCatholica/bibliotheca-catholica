What Did Augustine Mean by the Gold of Egypt?

One image from the Exodus, and the four things it was meant to teach

"Plundering the Egyptians" has become a shorthand for Christians using secular learning. The phrase is so familiar that it is easy to miss how carefully Augustine built the image. This exploration asks one narrow question: what exactly did he mean by it?

The story behind it

When the Israelites left Egypt, the Book of Exodus tells us, they asked their Egyptian neighbours for vessels of silver and gold and for clothing, and the Egyptians gave them (Exodus 3:22, 11:2, 12:35–36). Augustine was not the first to read this as a figure for learning. A century and a half earlier, Origen had written to his student Gregory that the Israelites made the vessels of the tabernacle from Egyptian gold, and that a Christian could likewise take from Greek philosophy "what may serve as a course of study or a preparation for Christianity."

Augustine took up the figure in Book II, chapter 40 of On Christian Doctrine, and gave it the form the tradition remembers. Read slowly, his paragraph makes four distinct points.

1. The gold was never theirs

The Egyptians' gold and silver, Augustine says, were things "which they did not create themselves, but dug out of the mines of God's providence which are everywhere scattered abroad." This is the heart of the image. Truth found in a pagan book is not a foreign possession that Christians acquire; it is God's, temporarily in other hands. He had already said so plainly in chapter 18: "wherever truth may be found, it belongs to his Master."

2. They took it by command

The Israelites did this "not doing this on their own authority, but by the command of God." Augustine is not licensing grabbing whatever seems useful. The taking is ordered to God's purpose: the Christian should "devote [it] to their proper use in preaching the gospel."

3. Gold, and also garments

The image is not only about ideas. Augustine adds the Egyptians' clothing: "human institutions such as are adapted to that intercourse with men which is indispensable in this life." Grammar, writing, law, the ordinary arts of a civilized life: these too can be taken over and put to Christian use. He then names those who had done it, from Cyprian and Lactantius to Hilary, and, before all of them, Moses, who "was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians" (Acts 7:22).

4. The gold can also make a calf

Here is the point most often left out. The same Exodus story includes the golden calf. Origen had already warned that "not many take from Egypt only the useful," and that some "from their Greek studies, produce heretical notions, and set them up, like the golden calf."

Augustine knew this from his own life. In the Confessions he describes reading the books of the Platonists and using the same image of himself:

I set my mind upon the gold which Thou willedst Thy people to take from Egypt, seeing Thine it was, wheresoever it were.

But in the next breath he adds that he did not set his mind "on the idols of Egypt, whom they served with Thy gold." Gold carried out of Egypt can furnish the tabernacle or be melted into an idol. The metal is the same; the use is what differs. That is why, directly after the gold of Egypt, On Christian Doctrine turns to humility and the Passover.

So what did he mean?

In short: truth is God's wherever it turns up; Christians may take it, under obedience, for God's service; this includes the ordinary arts as well as philosophy; and the reader must watch that the gold is not turned into a new idol.

It is worth adding that Augustine offers this reading of Exodus "without prejudice to any other interpretation that may be as good, or better." It is a Father's counsel, widely loved, not a definition of faith.
