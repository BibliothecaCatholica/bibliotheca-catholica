What Did Boethius Mean by an "Eternal Present"?

Why God's knowing the future is more like seeing than predicting

One of the hardest knots in Christian thought is this: if God knows today what I will choose tomorrow, how can tomorrow's choice be free? Boethius, writing in prison in the sixth century, untied it with one idea, which he puts in the mouth of Lady Philosophy in the last book of The Consolation of Philosophy. This exploration asks only what that idea is.

Eternity is not a very long time

We usually picture eternity as time that never ends: an endless line running forward. Boethius says that is the wrong picture. His definition:

Now, eternity is the possession of endless life whole and perfect at a single moment.

— Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book V, prose 6

Everything in time, he explains, lives one moment at a time: "To-morrow's state it grasps not yet, while it has already lost yesterday's." Even a world that had no beginning and no end, as some philosophers imagined, would still be living moment by moment. It would be endless, but not eternal. Hence his neat distinction: "God indeed is eternal, but the world everlasting."

God does not move along the line of time at all. He possesses His whole life at once.

Knowledge "of a moment that never passes"

If that is what God's life is like, then His knowledge is like it too. God does not look ahead to our future, as a forecaster does. He sees all times as present:

thou wilt more rightly deem it not foreknowledge as of something future, but knowledge of a moment that never passes.

— Consolation, Book V, prose 6

Boethius even draws a lesson from the word itself. What God has is better called "not prevision, but providence," because "its outlook embraces all things as from some lofty height."

Seeing does not force

Now the key step. Philosophy asks Boethius a simple question: when you watch something happen, does your watching make it happen?

Why, then, dost thou insist that the things which are surveyed by the Divine eye are involved in necessity, whereas clearly men impose no necessity on things which they see? Does the act of vision add any necessity to the things which thou seest before thy eyes?

"Assuredly not," he answers.

She then gives an example. When you look out and see, at the same moment, a man walking and the sun rising, you take in both at a glance. Yet you know the difference between them: you judge "the former voluntary, the latter necessary action." Seeing both at once does not turn the man's walk into something as fixed as the sunrise.

So it is with God. His vision takes in all things in His eternal present, but it "changes not the natures and properties of things." What is free is seen as free, and it remains free.

What the idea does, and does not, do

The eternal present answers one specific worry: that God's knowing our choices makes them unfree. It shows that knowledge as such, whether ours of the present or God's of all time, does not compel.

It does not, by itself, answer every question about grace, predestination, and freedom. Later theologians, St. Thomas Aquinas among them, took up Boethius's definition and built on it, and they debated the deeper questions for centuries. Boethius's image is a starting point, not the whole conversation.

It is also worth being clear that the eternal present is a philosophical explanation. What the Church teaches as a matter of faith is that God knows all things, governs all things by His providence, and that human beings are truly free (see the Catechism, paragraphs 302 to 314). Boethius offers one of the most helpful ways ever found of seeing how those truths fit together.
