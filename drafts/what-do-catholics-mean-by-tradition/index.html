What Do Catholics Mean by "Tradition"?

Not nostalgia, not a second Bible, but the living handing-on of the Gospel

"Tradition" is one of those words Catholics use constantly and explain rarely. To some people it suggests nostalgia: a fondness for the way things used to be done. To others it suggests a second, secret source of doctrine alongside the Bible. Neither is what the Church means. At heart the word means a handing-on, and what it describes is simple. Christ gave the Gospel to the apostles, and the apostles handed it on, by their preaching and their writing, to those who came after them.

This essay looks at what the Church teaches about Tradition, and at three early witnesses in the library who help us see it at work.

What the Church teaches

The clearest modern statement is the Second Vatican Council's constitution Dei Verbum, which the Catechism follows closely (paragraphs 75 to 83).

Tradition comes from the apostles. The Council teaches that the apostles handed on the Gospel in two ways. Some was handed on orally, by their preaching, their example, and the observances they established. Some was handed on in writing, by those who, under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, set the message of salvation down in the books of the New Testament. To keep the Gospel whole and alive, the apostles left bishops as their successors.

Scripture and Tradition belong together. Dei Verbum describes Scripture and Tradition as flowing from the same divine wellspring and tending toward the same end. Scripture is the word of God written under the inspiration of the Spirit. Tradition hands on that same word of God, entrusted to the apostles, to their successors. The Church therefore does not draw her certainty about everything revealed from Scripture alone, and both are to be received with the same reverence.

The teaching office serves the word. The task of authentically interpreting the word of God belongs to the Church's teaching office. But the Council adds at once that this office is not above the word of God. It serves it, teaching only what has been handed on.

The apostles themselves pointed to it. Dei Verbum recalls that the apostles, handing on what they had received, warned the faithful to hold fast to the traditions they had learned, whether by word of mouth or by letter. St. Paul's words, as St. Basil quoted them in the fourth century, are "Hold fast the traditions which ye have been taught whether by word, or our Epistle" (2 Thessalonians 2:15). The New Testament does not present itself as the only way the Gospel was handed on.

Tradition grows in understanding. Dei Verbum also says that the Tradition from the apostles develops in the Church with the help of the Holy Spirit. This is not by adding new revelation. It is by a growth in understanding of what was handed down.

Irenaeus: you can check it

In the second century, St. Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 130–202) was writing against Gnostic teachers who claimed to possess secret doctrines passed on privately from the apostles. His answer was an appeal to something public:

It is within the power of all, therefore, in every Church, who may wish to see the truth, to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles manifested throughout the whole world.

— St. Irenaeus, Against Heresies, Book III, ch. 3

If the apostles had secret teachings, he argues, they would have given them to the men they entrusted with the churches. And those men are known by name, in an unbroken line. He gives his own example. As a young man he had seen St. Polycarp, who had been taught by apostles, and who died, Irenaeus says, "having always taught the things which he had learned from the apostles, and which the Church has handed down, and which alone are true."

For Irenaeus, then, Tradition is the opposite of secret. It is the public, continuous teaching of the churches founded by the apostles, which anyone can examine.

Vincent: growth without change of meaning

In the fifth century, St. Vincent of Lérins (d. c. 445) faced a different question. If the faith is handed on unchanged, can there be any progress in it at all? He answers yes, and offers an image that has never been bettered:

The growth of religion in the soul must be analogous to the growth of the body, which, though in process of years it is developed and attains its full size, yet remains still the same.

— St. Vincent of Lérins, Commonitory, ch. 23

A child and the grown man are the same person, Vincent says. Nothing appears in the adult that was not already present, in some way, in the child. So with doctrine. The Church's understanding deepens and its expression becomes fuller, but it does not turn into something else. Newman: the long view

In the nineteenth century, John Henry Newman took up Vincent's image and spent a whole book testing it against the history of the Church. His Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine (1845) proposed ways of telling a faithful development from a corruption. By the time the book was printed, he had been received into the Catholic Church: a postscript explains that, while it was in the press, he became convinced of "the truth of the conclusion to which the discussion leads." This week's Book of the Week introduces it.

Tradition and traditions

One more distinction matters. The Catechism (paragraph 83) distinguishes the apostolic Tradition from the many theological, disciplinary, liturgical, and devotional traditions that have grown up in local churches over time. These smaller traditions are particular ways in which the great Tradition is expressed. Under the guidance of the Church's teaching office, they can be kept, adapted, or even set aside.

So when a Catholic says "Tradition," he should not mean every custom he grew up with. He means the faith of the apostles, handed on and kept whole. (This week's other exploration takes up that distinction directly.)

Why it matters

This is not an abstract question. It shapes how a Catholic reads the Bible: within the living faith of the Church that first received and recognized it, not against it. It shapes how we read the Fathers: as witnesses to what was handed down, not as a collection of private opinions. And it shapes how we live with change. Some things in the Church's life do change, and should. Others cannot, because they are not ours to change. Knowing the difference between Tradition and traditions is the beginning of peace on that point.

What the Church teaches, and what this essay argues

Church teaching. Everything in the section "What the Church teaches," and the Tradition/traditions distinction, is drawn from Dei Verbum 7 to 10 and the Catechism 75 to 83. It is paraphrased rather than quoted.

The Fathers' witness. Irenaeus and Vincent are early witnesses to how the Church understood Tradition. The Catechism itself cites Irenaeus on apostolic succession. Their particular arguments and images are theirs, offered as witnesses, not as definitions.

Newman. Newman's tests for development are a theologian's proposal, widely respected and influential, but not themselves Church teaching.

This essay's framing. The opening contrast with "nostalgia" and a "second Bible" is my own way of introducing the subject.
