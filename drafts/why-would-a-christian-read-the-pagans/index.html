Why Would a Christian Read the Pagans?

Augustine, the gold of Egypt, and the freedom to learn from anyone who tells the truth

Somewhere in nearly every Catholic library sits a shelf of books by men who never heard of Christ. Plato, Aristotle, Cicero, Virgil. A reader who has just come to the Faith, or come back to it, may reasonably ask what those books are doing there. If we have the Gospels, the Fathers, and the saints, why spend our few reading hours on authors who worshipped idols, or worshipped nothing?

The question is old. Christians were asking it within a few generations of the apostles, and they did not all give the same answer. Looking at how the early Church argued it out is still the best way to answer it for ourselves.

Two answers from the early centuries

Around the year 200, Tertullian of Carthage put the case against in a sentence that has never stopped being quoted:

What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What concord is there between the Academy and the Church? What between heretics and Christians?

— Tertullian, The Prescription Against Heretics, ch. 7

Tertullian was not being lazy. He was a learned man writing against heretics who had, in his view, built their errors out of borrowed philosophy. He had watched clever arguments carry people away from the creed, and he wanted a wall.

Half a century earlier, St. Justin Martyr had answered the other way. Justin had been a philosopher before he was a Christian, and he did not think his conversion required him to call everything he had learned worthless. In his Second Apology he wrote:

Whatever things were rightly said among all men, are the property of us Christians.

— St. Justin Martyr, Second Apology, ch. 13

Justin's reason is theological, not sentimental. The pagan writers, he says, "spoke well in proportion to the share he had of the spermatic word," the seed of the Word who would become flesh in Christ. What they saw, they saw "darkly." But it was real, and it belonged to the One who gave it.

So the tension is there from the start. One side fears contamination; the other sees scattered seeds of the truth. Both are trying to protect the same thing.

Augustine's turn

St. Augustine knew both instincts from the inside. In the Confessions he tells us that the book which first turned his heart toward God was not Scripture but a lost dialogue of Cicero, the Hortensius. He read it at nineteen, as a student of rhetoric:

But this book altered my affections, and turned my prayers to Thyself O Lord; and made me have other purposes and desires.

— St. Augustine, Confessions, Book III

Later, in the books of the Platonists, he found ideas about God and the soul that helped free him from the materialism that had held him for years. He is careful to say what those books lacked: the Incarnation, the humility of the cross, the grace that heals. But he does not pretend they gave him nothing.

When he became a bishop and sat down to write a handbook for Christian teachers, On Christian Doctrine, he had to decide what to tell young men who would preach and interpret Scripture. Should they read the pagans? His answer became one of the most influential passages on the subject in Christian history. He starts from a principle:

Nay, but let every good and true Christian understand that wherever truth may be found, it belongs to his Master.

— St. Augustine, On Christian Doctrine, Book II, ch. 18

Then, a few chapters later, he gives the image that the whole tradition would remember. When the Israelites left Egypt, God told them to take the Egyptians' gold and silver with them. Augustine reads this as a figure of what Christians should do with pagan learning:

Now these are, so to speak, their gold and silver, which they did not create themselves, but dug out of the mines of God's providence which are everywhere scattered abroad.

— St. Augustine, On Christian Doctrine, Book II, ch. 40

The phrase to notice is "which they did not create themselves." For Augustine, the truth in Plato was never Plato's property in the first place. It came out of God's mine. Reading it is not a raid on enemy territory; it is a recovery of what was always the Lord's.

(We will look more closely at the image itself, and at the Exodus story behind it, in this week's short exploration, "What Did Augustine Mean by the Gold of Egypt?")

The conditions Augustine attached

It would be easy to stop there and conclude that a Christian may read anything. Augustine does not stop there.

First, he distinguishes. Pagan learning contains "false and superstitious fancies and heavy burdens of unnecessary toil," which the Christian "ought to abhor and avoid," as well as "liberal instruction" and "some most excellent precepts of morality." The gold is real, and so are the idols. The reader has to tell them apart.

Second, he warns about the reader's own heart. Immediately after the gold of Egypt, he turns to St. Paul: "Knowledge puffs up, but charity edifies" (1 Cor. 8:1). Whatever riches a student brings out of Egypt, Augustine says, "unless he has kept the passover, he cannot be safe." Learning without humility is not a gain.

Third, he keeps the proportions straight. All the useful knowledge in the pagan books, he says, is poor next to Scripture, "just as poor as the store of gold and silver and garments which the people of Israel brought with them out of Egypt was in comparison with the riches which they afterwards attained at Jerusalem."

So Augustine's answer is neither Tertullian's wall nor an open door. It is a door with a threshold: read freely, discern carefully, stay humble, and keep the Scriptures at the center.

Why this still matters

Few of us are tempted to worship Jupiter. But the question has not gone away. It only changes shape. A Catholic today reads novelists who reject the Faith, historians who doubt the Gospels, philosophers who deny the soul. Our children read them in school. The question "should a Christian read the pagans?" becomes "should I read this book by someone who does not share my faith?"

Augustine gives us more than permission. He gives us a way of reading. We come to a book expecting that, if it contains any truth, that truth is God's and can be received with gratitude. We come knowing that it may also contain errors, which we are free to set down. And we come asking whether the reading is making us more charitable or only more clever.

That last test is the hardest. Augustine's own story shows why it matters. The Hortensius lit a fire in him, but it took years, and a great deal of grace, before that fire found its true object.

St. Thomas Aquinas, eight centuries later, would put the principle in his own terms. Sacred doctrine, he wrote, "makes use also of the authority of philosophers in those questions in which they were able to know the truth by natural reason," but treats those authorities "as extrinsic and probable arguments," not as proof of the Faith. The Christian reader learns from the philosopher without making him a prophet.

What the Church teaches, and what this essay argues

It is worth being clear about which parts of this are the Church's teaching and which are one reader's reflection.

Church teaching. The Church teaches that faith and reason come from the same God and cannot truly contradict each other, and that human reason, even without revelation, can come to know real truths about the world and about God. St. John Paul II developed this at length in his encyclical Fides et Ratio (1998), which is available in the library and repays a slow reading.

The Fathers' counsel. The "gold of Egypt" reading of Exodus is Augustine's interpretation, offered (as he himself says) "without prejudice to any other interpretation that may be as good, or better." It became widely shared, but it is a Father's counsel, not a definition of faith.

This essay's argument. The suggestion that Augustine's three conditions (discernment, humility, and the primacy of Scripture) are a practical guide for reading today is my own application, offered for your consideration and correction.

Where to start

If you would like to read the sources yourself, begin with Book II, chapters 18 to 42 of On Christian Doctrine. It is about an hour of reading, plain, and more practical than you might expect. Then read Book III of the Confessions for the Hortensius, and Book VII for the books of the Platonists. Justin's Second Apology takes less than an hour.
