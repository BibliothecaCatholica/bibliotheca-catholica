Reading plan · November 29 to December 24, 2026

Advent with the Fathers

Advent 2026 begins on Sunday, November 29, and ends on Christmas Eve. This plan gives one short reading for each of its 26 days, drawn only from the public-domain editions in our reading room. The first weeks look toward Christ’s coming at the end of time; from December 17 the readings follow the O Antiphons toward Christmas. Each link opens the reader at the start of the passage.

  1. Day 1Sunday, November 29about 2 min

    First Sunday of Advent

    He will come, and will not tarry

    Ye foolish ones! compare yourselves to a tree: take [for instance] the vine. First of all, it sheds its leaves, then it buds, next it puts forth leaves, and then it flowers; after that comes the sour grape, and then follows the ripened fruit. Ye perceive how in a little time the fruit of a tree comes to maturity. Of a truth, soon and suddenly shall His will be accomplished, as the Scripture also bears witness, saying, “Speedily will He come, and will not tarry;” and, “The Lord shall suddenly come to His temple, even the Holy One, for whom ye look.”

    St. Clement of Rome (fl. c. 96), Apostolic Father, First Epistle to the Corinthians, ch. XXIII. Ante-Nicene Christian Library, vol. I, trans. Roberts, Donaldson & Crombie (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1870).

    Already in the first century some Christians were tired of waiting: 'we have grown old, and none of them has happened unto us.' Clement answers them with a vine: leaf, bud, flower, sour grape, ripe fruit. God's promises ripen on His schedule, not ours, but they ripen. That is where Advent begins.

  2. Day 2Monday, November 30about 4 min

    St. Andrew, Apostle

    The last day, and the first coming

    That, therefore, which the whole Church of the true God holds and professes as its creed, that Christ shall come from heaven to judge quick and dead, this we call the last day, or last time, of the divine judgment. … For that day is properly called the day of judgment, because in it there shall be no room left for the ignorant questioning why this wicked person is happy and that righteous man unhappy.

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, The City of God, Book XX, ch. 1. Marcus Dods, ed. and trans., vol. II (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1871).

    Advent looks two ways: back to Bethlehem and forward to the day Christ 'shall come from heaven to judge quick and dead.' Augustine reminds us that this is simply the creed of the whole Church. It is also a comfort: on that day no one will be left asking why the wicked prosper and the good suffer. St. Andrew, whose feast is today, followed the Lamb at the first word, and he is a good companion for the waiting.

  3. Day 3Tuesday, December 1about 3 min

    Tuesday, First Week of Advent

    The restless heart

    Great art Thou, O Lord, and greatly to be praised; great is Thy power, and Thy wisdom infinite. And Thee would man praise; man, but a particle of Thy creation; man, that bears about him his mortality, the witness of his sin, the witness that Thou resistest the proud: yet would man praise Thee; he, but a particle of Thy creation. Thou awakest us to delight in Thy praise; for Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee. … My faith, Lord, shall call on Thee, which Thou hast given me, wherewith Thou hast inspired me, through the Incarnation of Thy Son, through the ministry of the Preacher.

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, Confessions, Book I, ch. 1. E. B. Pusey, trans. (first pub. Oxford: Library of the Fathers, 1838; hosted file names no edition year; Pusey d. 1882).

    Augustine begins his life story with a longing nothing in this world can settle. The restlessness of a busy December is not a failure to fix with more activity; it is the ache of a heart made for God. Notice where his faith comes from: 'through the Incarnation of Thy Son.' The answer to the restless heart is the Child we are waiting for.

  4. Day 4Wednesday, December 2about 3 min

    Wednesday, First Week of Advent

    The city of the soul has many gates

    with desire to see thee a true guardian of the city of thy soul. Oh, dearest son, this city has many gates! They are three--Memory, Intellect, and Will, and our Creator allows all of them to be battered, and sometimes opened by violence, except one--that is, Will. … Let us strive to hold good and zealous watch, keeping at the side of our Watch Free-will, the dog Conscience, who when anyone comes at the gate must awake Reason by its barking, that she may discern whether it be friend or foe

    St. Catherine of Siena (1347–1380), Doctor of the Church, Letters, Letter to Stefano Maconi. Vida D. Scudder, trans. and ed. (London: J. M. Dent, 1905).

    Catherine wrote this letter in Advent; she mentions arriving 'on the first Sunday in Advent with much peace.' Her picture is of a walled city: memory and intellect can be battered by every passing noise, but the will opens only if we consent. Advent's watchfulness is not anxiety about everything. It is keeping one gate well, with conscience awake beside it.

  5. Day 5Thursday, December 3about 2 min

    St. Francis Xavier

    Prepare a worthy mansion

    Christ will come to thee, and show thee His consolation, if thou prepare a worthy mansion for Him within thee. All His glory and beauty is from within, and there it pleaseth Him to dwell. … Go to, faithful soul, prepare thy heart for this bridegroom that he may vouchsafe to come to thee and dwell within thee, for so He saith, if any man loveth me he will keep my words: and my Father will love him, and we will come unto him and make our abode with him. Give, therefore, place to Christ and refuse entrance to all others.

    Thomas à Kempis (c. 1380–1471), The Imitation of Christ, Book II, ch. 1, "Of the inward life". William Benham, trans. (hosted file names no edition year; Benham d. 1910).

    'Christ will come to thee': for Thomas à Kempis, Advent is not only a date on the calendar but a visit to the soul. The preparation is simple and demanding: make room, and keep the room for Him. St. Francis Xavier, whose feast is today, crossed oceans so that others could receive that visit; most of us are asked to cross only the distance into our own hearts.

  6. Day 6Friday, December 4about 2 min

    Friday, First Week of Advent

    An evangelist before the Gospel

    Isaiah, then, together with his rebukes of wickedness, precepts of righteousness, and predictions of evil, also prophesied much more than the rest about Christ and the Church, that is, about the King and that city which he founded; so that some say he should be called an evangelist rather than a prophet. … But let us now hear what follows about the Church. He says, "Rejoice, O barren, thou that barest not; break forth and cry, thou that didst not travail with child: for many more are the children of the desolate than of her that has an husband."

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, The City of God, Book XVIII, ch. 29. Marcus Dods, ed. and trans., vol. II (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1871).

    Isaiah is the prophet of Advent; the Church reads him almost every day of the season. Augustine notes that some called him 'an evangelist rather than a prophet', because he saw the King and His city so clearly. And his word to the barren is joy: the God who comes makes the desolate fruitful.

  7. Day 7Saturday, December 5about 3 min

    Saturday, First Week of Advent

    When the wind rose, he prayed

    It is no wonder that he joyfully beheld the day of his death, or rather the day of the Lord, the coming whereof he had always been mindful to await with earnest expectation. … if it happened that there blew a sudden strong gust of wind, when he was reading or doing any other thing, he forthwith called upon the Lord for mercy, and begged that it might be granted to all mankind.

    St. Bede the Venerable (673–735), Doctor of the Church, Ecclesiastical History of the English People, Book IV, ch. 3 (St. Chad). A. M. Sellar, rev. trans. (London: George Bell and Sons, 1907).

    Bede remembers St. Chad, a bishop who lived 'mindful to await with earnest expectation' the day of the Lord. A gust of wind was enough to turn him to prayer, and he prayed for everyone, not only himself. Today, let ordinary interruptions (a siren, a storm, a notification) become small reminders that the Lord is coming.

  8. Day 8Sunday, December 6about 3 min

    Second Sunday of Advent

    Sent to persuade, not to compel

    Was it then, as one might conceive, for the purpose of exercising tyranny, or of inspiring fear and terror? By no means, but under the influence of clemency and meekness. As a king sends his son, who is also a king, so sent He Him; as God He sent Him; as to men He sent Him; as a Saviour He sent Him, and as seeking to persuade, not to compel us; for violence has no place in the character of God. As calling us He sent Him, not as vengefully pursuing us; as loving us He sent Him, not as judging us. For He will yet send Him to judge us, and who shall endure His appearing?

    Epistle to Diognetus (anonymous, 2nd century), Epistle to Diognetus, ch. VII. Ante-Nicene Christian Library, vol. I, trans. Roberts, Donaldson & Crombie (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1870).

    Why does God come as a child and not as a conqueror? This unknown second-century writer answers: because violence has no place in the character of God. He comes calling, not pursuing; loving, not yet judging. Advent holds both comings together: the gentle one we celebrate, and the one we prepare for.

  9. Day 9Monday, December 7about 3 min

    St. Ambrose

    Led by a bishop to God

    To Milan I came, to Ambrose the Bishop, known to the whole world as among the best of men, Thy devout servant; whose eloquent discourse did then plentifully dispense unto Thy people the flour of Thy wheat, the gladness of Thy oil, and the sober inebriation of Thy wine. To him was I unknowing led by Thee, that by him I might knowingly be led to Thee. That man of God received me as a father, and showed me an Episcopal kindness on my coming. Thenceforth I began to love him, at first indeed not as a teacher of the truth (which I utterly despaired of in Thy Church), but as a person kind towards myself. … and yet was I drawing nearer by little and little, and unconsciously.

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, Confessions, Book V, ch. 13–14. E. B. Pusey, trans. (first pub. Oxford: Library of the Fathers, 1838; hosted file names no edition year; Pusey d. 1882).

    On St. Ambrose's feast, Augustine remembers meeting him. Before Ambrose's teaching won him, his kindness did: he 'received me as a father.' And Augustine was drawing nearer 'by little and little, and unconsciously.' Many people come to Christ that way. Who has God put in your path this Advent to receive kindly?

  10. Day 10Tuesday, December 8about 2 min

    Immaculate Conception

    Preserved for the second Adam

    But God ordained that the blessed Virgin should be exempt from this common calamity, for he had destined her to be the mother of the second Adam, Jesus Christ, who was to repair the injury done by the first. Now, let us see how fitting it was that the Three Divine Persons should preserve this Virgin from original sin. We shall see that it was befitting the Father to preserve her from it as his daughter, the Son as his mother, the Holy Spirit as his spouse.

    St. Alphonsus Liguori (1696–1787), Doctor of the Church, The Glories of Mary, Discourse on the Immaculate Conception. Anonymous English translation, 2nd American ed. (New York: Edward Dunigan and Brother, 1852); translator not named.

    Two years before the dogma was defined in 1854, this American edition of St. Alphonsus was already teaching what the Church believes: Mary was preserved from original sin so that she could be the mother of the One who repairs it. Today's feast is really about how carefully God prepared for His coming, and how He prepares a place in each of us.

  11. Day 11Wednesday, December 9about 3 min

    St. Juan Diego

    O sweet exchange!

    He Himself took on Him the burden of our iniquities, He gave His own Son as a ransom for us, the holy One for transgressors, the blameless One for the wicked, the righteous One for the unrighteous, the incorruptible One for the corruptible, the immortal One for them that are mortal. For what other thing was capable of covering our sins than His righteousness? By what other one was it possible that we, the wicked and ungodly, could be justified, than by the only Son of God? O sweet exchange! O unsearchable operation! O benefits surpassing all expectation! that the wickedness of many should be hid in a single righteous One, and that the righteousness of One should justify many transgressors!

    Epistle to Diognetus (anonymous, 2nd century), Epistle to Diognetus, ch. IX. Ante-Nicene Christian Library, vol. I, trans. Roberts, Donaldson & Crombie (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1870).

    'O sweet exchange!' This is one of the earliest Christian hymns to the meaning of the Incarnation: the holy One for transgressors, the immortal for the mortal. God waited until our need was plain, and then gave His own Son. St. Juan Diego, whose feast is today, called himself a nobody; the sweet exchange is meant for exactly such people.

  12. Day 12Thursday, December 10about 5 min

    Thursday, Second Week of Advent

    Thy kingdom come

    And since prayer is, in a sort, the interpreter of our desires before God, we can only rightly ask in prayer for those things which we can rightly desire. But in the Lord's Prayer not only do we have petitions for all those things which we can rightly desire, but they are set forth in the order in which they are to be desired. Hence this prayer not only teaches us how to pray, but serves as the norm of all our dispositions of mind. … And when we say, Thy kingdom come, it is not as though we meant that God did not reign, but, as S. Augustine says to Proba: "We stir up our desires for that kingdom, that it may come upon us and that we may reign in it."

    St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), Doctor of the Church, On Prayer and the Contemplative Life (from the Summa, II-II q. 83), On the seven petitions of the Lord’s Prayer. Hugh Pope, O.P., trans. (London: R. & T. Washbourne, 1914).

    Advent is a season of desire, and Aquinas says prayer is how desire speaks to God. The Our Father does more than voice our wants; it teaches us what to want, and in what order. When we pray 'Thy kingdom come' we are not informing God of anything; we are stirring up our own longing. Pray it slowly today.

  13. Day 13Friday, December 11about 2 min

    Friday, Second Week of Advent

    And thou, Bethlehem

    This prophet predicts the very place in which Christ was born, saying, "And thou, Bethlehem, of the house of Ephratah, art the least that can be reckoned among the thousands of Judah; out of thee shall come forth unto me a leader, to be the prince in Israel; and His going forth is from the beginning, even from the days of eternity.

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, The City of God, Book XVIII, ch. 30 (the prophet Micah). Marcus Dods, ed. and trans., vol. II (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1871).

    Micah names the town: Bethlehem, 'the least that can be reckoned among the thousands of Judah.' The One whose 'going forth is from the beginning, even from the days of eternity' chooses to be born in the smallest place. Augustine sets this prophecy alongside Christ's birth, and Advent invites us to do the same: to look for God in the least places, including our own.

  14. Day 14Saturday, December 12about 1 min

    Our Lady of Guadalupe

    A simple maid and a meek

    In this [Shewing] He brought our blessed Lady to my understanding. I saw her ghostly, in bodily likeness: a simple maid and a meek, young of age and little waxen above a child, in the stature that she was when she conceived. Also God shewed in part the wisdom and the truth of her soul: wherein I understood the reverent beholding in which she beheld her God and Maker, marvelling with great reverence that He would be born of her that was a simple creature of His making. And this wisdom and truth: knowing the greatness of her Maker and the littleness of herself that was made,--caused her to say full meekly to Gabriel: Lo me, God's handmaid!

    Julian of Norwich (c. 1343–after 1416), Revelations of Divine Love, ch. IV. Grace Warrack, ed. (London: Methuen, 1901).

    Julian sees Mary as she was at the Annunciation: young, 'little waxen above a child', marvelling that her Maker would be born of her. On the feast of Our Lady of Guadalupe, who appeared as a young mother to a poor man at Tepeyac, we meet the same humility. Her greatness is that she knows her own littleness, and says yes.

  15. Day 15Sunday, December 13about 3 min

    Third Sunday of Advent (Gaudete)

    Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Sion

    So we see all nations moved to the faith; and the fulfilment of what follows, "And the desired of all nations shall come," is looked for at His last coming. For ere men can desire and wait for Him, they must believe and love Him. … Zechariah says of Christ and the Church, "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Sion; shout joyfully, O daughter of Jerusalem: behold, thy King shall come unto thee, just and the Saviour; Himself poor, and mounting an ass, and a colt the foal of an ass: and His dominion shall be from sea to sea, and from the river even to the ends of the earth."

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, The City of God, Book XVIII, ch. 35 (Haggai and Zechariah). Marcus Dods, ed. and trans., vol. II (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1871).

    Gaudete Sunday: rejoice, the Lord is near. Zechariah's 'Rejoice greatly' is the joy of a city whose King is coming, humble and riding on a colt. Augustine adds a quiet line worth keeping all week: 'ere men can desire and wait for Him, they must believe and love Him.' Joy, desire, and faith grow together.

  16. Day 16Monday, December 14about 4 min

    St. John of the Cross

    Time and eternity

    Now, eternity is the possession of endless life whole and perfect at a single moment. What this is becomes more clear and manifest from a comparison with things temporal. For whatever lives in time is a present proceeding from the past to the future, and there is nothing set in time which can embrace the whole space of its life together. To-morrow's state it grasps not yet, while it has already lost yesterday's; nay, even in the life of to-day ye live no longer than one brief transitory moment.

    Boethius (c. 477–524), The Consolation of Philosophy, Book V, Prose VI. H. R. James, trans. (London: Elliot Stock, 1897).

    Written in prison while awaiting execution, this is the classic Christian definition of eternity: all of life, at once, with nothing lost. We live in pieces, one moment at a time; God does not. St. John of the Cross, whose feast is today, also wrote in a prison cell and found God there. The Eternal entering time is what Advent waits for, and why our brief moments matter to Him.

  17. Day 17Tuesday, December 15about 6 min

    Tuesday, Third Week of Advent

    The Lord whom ye seek shall suddenly come

    Again he says of Him, "Behold I will send mine angel, and He will look out the way before my face: and the Lord, whom ye seek, shall suddenly come into His temple, even the Angel of the testament, whom ye desire. Behold, He cometh, saith the Lord Almighty, and who shall abide the day of His entry, or who shall stand at His appearing?" In this place he has foretold both the first and second advent of Christ:

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, The City of God, Book XVIII, ch. 35 (Malachi). Marcus Dods, ed. and trans., vol. II (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1871).

    Malachi is the last page of the Old Testament, and here Augustine finds both advents in one sentence: the Lord coming suddenly into His temple, and the day when 'who shall stand at His appearing?' The temple, he says, is Christ's own flesh. The One we seek is the One who comes to seek us.

  18. Day 18Wednesday, December 16about 2 min

    Wednesday, Third Week of Advent

    The power of the Most High shall overshadow thee

    This shadow, behind which the power of God effects the entrance and growth of Jesus Christ in our souls, is the form assumed by the duties, attractions, and crosses of each moment. … O bread of angels! Heavenly manna! Pearl of the Gospel! Sacrament of the present moment! Thou givest God under appearances as poor and mean as the manger, the hay, and the straw! But to whom dost thou give Him? Esurientes reples bonis. God reveals Himself to the humble in little things; and the proud, regarding only the exterior, find Him not even in great things.

    Jean-Pierre de Caussade, S.J. (1675–1751), Abandonment to Divine Providence, Book I, ch. II. Ella McMahon, trans., ed. H. Ramière (New York: Benziger Brothers, 1887).

    Caussade reads the Annunciation as a pattern for every day: God overshadows us through the ordinary duties and crosses of the present moment. Mary's life was 'very simple, very ordinary', and God was working great things inside it. ('Esurientes reples bonis', from the Magnificat: 'He hath filled the hungry with good things.') Look for Him today in the manger-plain things in front of you.

  19. Day 19Thursday, December 17about 1 min

    O Sapientia

    O loving wisdom of our God

    Praise to the Holiest in the height,
    And in the depth be praise:
    In all His words most wonderful;
    Most sure in all His ways!

    O loving wisdom of our God!
    When all was sin and shame,
    A second Adam to the fight
    And to the rescue came.

    O wisest love! that flesh and blood
    Which did in Adam fail,
    Should strive afresh against the foe,
    Should strive and should prevail;

    And that a higher gift than grace
    Should flesh and blood refine,
    God's Presence and His very Self,
    And Essence all divine.

    St. John Henry Newman (1801–1890), Doctor of the Church, The Dream of Gerontius, Fifth Choir of Angelicals. Longmans, Green and Co. school edition, 1910 printing (poem first pub. 1865).

    Today the Church begins the O Antiphons, the ancient evening cries of Advent's last week. The first is 'O Wisdom.' Newman's angels sing what that Wisdom did: when all was sin and shame, it sent a second Adam. The plan of salvation is not cold logic. It is 'wisest love.'

  20. Day 20Friday, December 18about 3 min

    O Adonai

    He was seen on the earth

    Also in another place he says, "This is my God, and there shall none other be accounted of in comparison of Him; who hath found out all the way of prudence, and hath given it to Jacob His servant, and to Israel His beloved: afterward He was seen on the earth, and conversed with men." … Again the same prophet says concerning Him, "Behold the days come, saith the Lord, that I will raise up unto David a righteous shoot, and a King shall reign and shall be wise, and shall do judgment and justice in the earth. In those days Judah shall be saved, and Israel shall dwell confidently: and this is the name which they shall call Him, Our righteous Lord."

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, The City of God, Book XVIII, ch. 33 (Jeremiah and Baruch). Marcus Dods, ed. and trans., vol. II (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1871).

    'O Adonai, Lord and Ruler of the house of Israel, who gave the law on Sinai.' The prophet's line is astonishing: the God who gave the law 'was seen on the earth, and conversed with men.' The righteous shoot of David will be called 'Our righteous Lord.' The Lawgiver comes to walk and talk with us.

  21. Day 21Saturday, December 19about 3 min

    O Radix Jesse

    Behold the handmaid of the Lord

    O Lord my God, my Creator and Redeemer! with such affection, reverence, praise, and honour, with such gratitude, worthiness, and love, with such faith, hope, and purity do I desire to receive Thee this day, as Thy most blessed Mother, the glorious Virgin Mary, received and desired Thee, when she humbly and devoutly answered the Angel who brought unto her the glad tidings of the mystery of the Incarnation. Behold the handmaid of the Lord; be it unto me according to thy word.

    Thomas à Kempis (c. 1380–1471), The Imitation of Christ, Book IV, ch. 17. William Benham, trans. (hosted file names no edition year; Benham d. 1910).

    'O Root of Jesse, standing as a sign for the peoples.' The old royal family tree of David had been cut to a stump; from it God raised a shoot through a young woman of Nazareth. Thomas à Kempis asks to receive Christ as Mary did, with faith, hope, and purity, in a single humble yes. That is how the Root flowers in us.

  22. Day 22Sunday, December 20about 2 min

    Fourth Sunday of Advent; O Clavis David

    Virgin Mother, daughter of thy Son

    “Thou Virgin Mother, daughter of thy Son,
    Humble and high beyond all other creature,
    The limit fixed of the eternal counsel,

    Thou art the one who such nobility
    To human nature gave, that its Creator
    Did not disdain to make himself its creature.

    Within thy womb rekindled was the love,
    By heat of which in the eternal peace
    After such wise this flower has germinated.

    Here unto us thou art a noonday torch
    Of charity, and below there among mortals
    Thou art the living fountain-head of hope.

    Lady, thou art so great, and so prevailing,
    That he who wishes grace, nor runs to thee,
    His aspirations without wings would fly.

    Dante Alighieri (1265–1321), The Divine Comedy: Paradiso, Canto XXXIII, lines 1–15 (St. Bernard's prayer to the Virgin). Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, trans. (first pub. Boston: Ticknor and Fields, 1867; hosted file names no edition year).

    On the last Sunday of Advent, Dante gives St. Bernard, the great Advent preacher, the most famous prayer to Mary in literature. 'Daughter of thy Son': the creature who would bear her Creator. Her 'yes' is the key (O Clavis David) that opens the door we could not open ourselves.

  23. Day 23Monday, December 21about 1 min

    O Oriens

    A star above all stars

    Now the virginity of Mary was hidden from the prince of this world, as was also her offspring, and the death of the Lord; three mysteries of renown, which were wrought in silence by God. How, then, was He manifested to the world? A star shone forth in heaven above all the other stars, the light of which was inexpressible, while its novelty struck men with astonishment. And all the rest of the stars, with the sun and moon, formed a chorus to this star, and its light was exceedingly great above them all. And there was agitation felt as to whence this new spectacle came, so unlike to everything else [in the heavens]. Hence every kind of magic was destroyed, and every bond of wickedness disappeared; ignorance was removed, and the old kingdom abolished, God Himself being manifested in human form for the renewal of eternal life.

    St. Ignatius of Antioch (d. c. 107), Apostolic Father, Epistle to the Ephesians (shorter version), ch. XIX. Ante-Nicene Christian Library, vol. I, trans. Roberts, Donaldson & Crombie (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1870).

    'O Dayspring, splendour of light eternal, come and enlighten those who sit in darkness.' On the darkest day of the year in the northern hemisphere, Ignatius, writing barely seventy years after the Resurrection, tells of a star brighter than all the others. Three mysteries were 'wrought in silence by God.' God still prefers to come quietly, and His light still outshines the rest.

  24. Day 24Tuesday, December 22about 3 min

    O Rex Gentium

    The desired of all nations

    For then "shall come the desired of all nations," as we read in the Hebrew. For before His advent He had not yet been desired by all nations. For they knew not Him whom they ought to desire, in whom they had not believed. Then, also, according to the Septuagint interpretation (for it also is a prophetic meaning), "shall come those who are elected of the Lord out of all nations."

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, The City of God, Book XVIII, ch. 48. Marcus Dods, ed. and trans., vol. II (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1871).

    'O King of the nations, and their desired One.' Augustine notices a paradox in Haggai's prophecy: the nations could not desire a Saviour they did not yet know. Advent is God awakening a desire in the world, and in us, that only He can then fulfil. And He gathers His people 'out of all nations.'

  25. Day 25Wednesday, December 23about 4 min

    O Emmanuel

    God with us

    While Herod, therefore, reigned in Judea, and Cæsar Augustus was emperor at Rome, the state of the republic being already changed, and the world being set at peace by him, Christ was born in Bethlehem of Judah, man manifest out of a human virgin, God hidden out of God the Father. For so had the prophet foretold: "Behold, a virgin shall conceive in the womb, and bring forth a Son, and they shall call His name Immanuel, which, being interpreted, is, God with us."

    St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Father and Doctor, The City of God, Book XVIII, ch. 46. Marcus Dods, ed. and trans., vol. II (Edinburgh: T. & T. Clark, 1871).

    The last O Antiphon is the plainest: Emmanuel, God with us. Augustine sets the birth in real history, under Herod and Caesar Augustus, in a world briefly at peace. 'Man manifest out of a human virgin, God hidden out of God the Father': one sentence holds the whole mystery we will kneel before in two days.

  26. Day 26Thursday, December 24about 2 min

    Christmas Eve

    Floods of light

    On that blessed night the sweet Infant Jesus, scarce an hour old, filled the darkness of my soul with floods of light. By becoming weak and little, for love of me, He made me strong and brave; He put His own weapons into my hands, so that I went from victory to victory, beginning, if I may say so, "to run as a giant." The fountain of my tears was dried up, and from that time they flowed neither easily nor often. … But Thérèse was no longer the same--Jesus had changed her heart.

    St. Thérèse of Lisieux (1873–1897), Doctor of the Church, Story of a Soul, ch. V. T. N. Taylor, ed. and trans. (London: Burns, Oates & Washbourne, 1912; 8th ed., 1922).

    Our last reading is about a Christmas Eve. At thirteen, coming home from Midnight Mass in 1886, Thérèse received what she called her 'complete conversion.' 'By becoming weak and little, for love of me, He made me strong.' Whatever you bring to the manger tonight, He can do the same. Merry Christmas, and thank you for keeping Advent with us.
